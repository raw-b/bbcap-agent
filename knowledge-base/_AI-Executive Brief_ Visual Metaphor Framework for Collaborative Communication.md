# **Executive Brief: Visual Metaphor Framework for Collaborative Communication**

**Source:** "Want to Give a Great Presentation? Use Ugly Sketches" — Martin J. Eppler, TED Talk  
 **Application:** Cross-platform LLM reference for generating visual metaphors in strategic communication, presentations, and collaborative problem-solving

---

## **Core Thesis**

Visual metaphors outperform data-heavy presentations and bullet points for communication, recall, and collaborative engagement. The effectiveness of these visuals depends not on polish, but on their provisional nature, metaphorical resonance, and capacity for variation.

---

## **Three Governing Principles**

### **Principle 1: Provisionality Over Polish**

Highly finished visuals create a "museum effect" — audiences admire rather than contribute. Rough, sketchy visuals signal "work in progress" and invite collaboration.

**Key Term:** Low Perceived Finishedness

**Application Logic:** When generating visual concepts, favor hand-drawn aesthetics, incomplete structures, or deliberately imperfect elements. This lowers psychological barriers to audience participation and co-creation.

**Supporting Evidence:** "Pencil selling" (sketching ideas with clients rather than presenting polished slides) correlates with higher sales conversion in B2B contexts across banking, telecom, and insurance sectors.

---

### **Principle 2: Metaphor Selection Criteria**

Visual metaphors work because they activate existing mental models. A well-chosen metaphor compresses complex information into an immediately graspable image.

**Selection Requirements:**

| Criterion | Definition | Test Question |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Simplicity | The metaphor must be simpler than the concept it explains | Does a child understand the image even without the label? |
| Concreteness | The metaphor references tangible, physical reality | Can you draw it in under 30 seconds? |
| Audience Fit | The metaphor resonates with the specific audience's experience | Could this image cause confusion or offense in this context? |

**Example from Talk:** A multi-billion dollar market analysis was conveyed through a single image — a "fortress built on sand." The fortress represented barriers to market entry (patents, scale, contracts). The sand represented the eroding value of the market as technology shifted. This replaced 40 data slides.

---

### **Principle 3: Visual Variation (Series Logic)**

Presenting a metaphor in variations encourages audiences to extend the pattern themselves. This aligns with variation theory: understanding deepens when a concept is seen from multiple angles.

**Application:** Once a base metaphor is established, generate 3-6 variations that explore different dimensions of the same conceptual space.

**Bridge Metaphor Example (Communication Problems):**

| Variation | Visual Description | Concept Represented |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| One-way bridge | Single direction traffic sign, one car crossing | Lack of listening; communication without dialogue |
| Misaligned bridge | Two bridge halves that don't connect | Misunderstanding; interpretation gaps |
| Overcrowded bridge | Multiple cars honking, gridlock | Information overload; key messages drowned out |

**Career Trajectory Example (Professional Path Archetypes):**

| Variation | Visual Shape | Concept Represented |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Traditional career | Stair-step ascending line | Steady upward progression |
| Late bloomer | Flat line then steep upward curve | Delayed but accelerating growth |
| Burnout | Upward curve with sharp downward hook | Unsustainable early success |
| Rollercoaster | Oscillating wave with forward direction | High volatility career |
| Radical reorientation | Line going one direction, then pivoting sharply | Career pivot or reinvention |
| Returning to what you like | Line dipping then rising parallel to original | Circling back to earlier passion |

## **Metaphor Categories (Generative Framework)**

When generating visual metaphors, draw from these archetypal categories:

### **Structural Metaphors**

Represent systems, organizations, or complex arrangements.

Examples: Buildings, bridges, fortresses, scaffolding, foundations, architecture

### **Journey Metaphors**

Represent processes, strategies, or progressions over time.

Examples: Mountain trails, roads, rivers, paths, stairways, labyrinths

### **Natural/Organic Metaphors**

Represent growth, ecosystems, or interdependent relationships.

Examples: Trees (roots, branches), gardens, weather patterns, seasons, ecosystems

### **Mechanical Metaphors**

Represent systems with inputs, outputs, and interdependent parts.

Examples: Gears, engines, pipelines, circuits, levers, pulleys

### **Container Metaphors**

Represent boundaries, capacity, or inclusion/exclusion dynamics.

Examples: Boxes, vessels, rooms, funnels, silos, bottles

## **Implementation Guidance for LLM Platforms**

### **When Generating Visual Metaphor Recommendations**

1. **Identify the abstract concept** the user wants to communicate  
2. **Match to metaphor category** based on concept type (system → structural; process → journey; relationship → organic)  
3. **Apply selection criteria** (simpler than concept, concrete, audience-appropriate)  
4. **Generate base metaphor** with brief description  
5. **Produce 3-5 variations** that explore different dimensions or scenarios  
6. **Flag provisionality** — suggest sketch-style execution over polished graphics

### **When Describing Visual Concepts**

Use concrete, drawable language. Describe what someone would see, not what it "represents." Let the conceptual mapping be self-evident or stated separately.

**Weak:** "An image representing the fragility of market position"  
 **Strong:** "A medieval fortress with thick walls, sitting on a sand dune — some walls already tilting as the sand shifts beneath them"
